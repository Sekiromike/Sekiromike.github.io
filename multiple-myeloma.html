Multiple myeloma

The biology, epidemiology, and treatment landscape behind my Myeloma Forecast Studio model. I wrote this as a short primer so the model's assumptions have some context.

Open the forecast model ↗ Source code

Pathophysiology

Multiple myeloma is a cancer of plasma cells, the antibody-producing cells of the bone marrow. It is genetically heterogeneous: different patients carry different chromosomal changes, which affects how the disease behaves. The malignant cells usually secrete a single monoclonal immunoglobulin (M-protein) or free light chains, and these serve as the main markers for diagnosis and monitoring.

The disease depends on its surroundings. Myeloma cells and bone marrow stromal cells signal to each other through cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and RANKL. That interaction drives the clinical features usually summarized by the CRAB criteria:

  • Calcium elevation: serum calcium above 11 mg/dL, from increased osteoclast activity.
  • Renal insufficiency: creatinine above 2 mg/dL, often from light-chain cast nephropathy.
  • Anemia: hemoglobin below 10 g/dL, from marrow infiltration and suppressed red cell production.
  • Bone lesions: lytic lesions that cause pain and fractures.

Epidemiology

MM accounts for roughly 10% of hematologic malignancies and 1.8% of all new US cancers. Incidence rises steeply with age; the median age at diagnosis is 69. SEER reports an age-adjusted incidence of 7.3 per 100,000 per year (2018–2022) and 5-year relative survival of 62.4% (2015–2021). An estimated 192,144 Americans were living with myeloma in 2022. The American Cancer Society projects about 36,000 new cases and 10,850 deaths in 2026. Incidence in Black Americans is more than twice that in White Americans.

Treatment Landscape (October 2026)

Care has moved from alkylating agents to multi-mechanism combinations, and T-cell–redirecting therapies are now moving into earlier lines:

  • Proteasome inhibitors (PIs): bortezomib, carfilzomib, ixazomib. They disrupt protein homeostasis in plasma cells.
  • Immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs): lenalidomide, pomalidomide. They drive cereblon-mediated degradation of Ikaros/Aiolos.
  • Anti-CD38 quadruplets as first-line standard: D-VRd for transplant-eligible patients (PERSEUS; FDA 2024) and for transplant-ineligible patients (CEPHEUS; FDA January 2026), and Isa-VRd for transplant-ineligible patients (IMROZ; FDA 2024).
  • BCMA CAR-T: cilta-cel from first relapse (CARTITUDE-4) and ide-cel from the third line (KarMMa-3).
  • Bispecific antibodies: teclistamab, elranatamab and linvoseltamab (BCMA) and talquetamab (GPRC5D). Teclistamab + daratumumab became the first bispecific approved at first relapse (MajesTEC-3; FDA March 2026).
  • Antibody–drug conjugate: belantamab mafodotin returned in combination with bortezomib/dexamethasone (DREAMM-7; FDA October 2025).

Forecast Highlights (base case)

The Multiple Myeloma Forecast Studio combines CDC U.S. Cancer Statistics incidence (1999–2022) with U.S. Census Bureau 2023 population projections. It simulates line-of-therapy patient flow to 2035 using Weibull PFS curves calibrated to pivotal trials. In the base case:

  • About 33,900 new diagnoses in 2026, rising to about 38,900 by 2035. Almost all of the growth comes from population ageing.
  • About 155,000 patients on active therapy in 2026, rising to about 236,000 by 2035. Quadruplet induction and continuous maintenance keep patients on first line longer.
  • About 55% of treated patients receive an anti-CD38 regimen in 2026. CAR-T and bispecific therapies are projected to reach about 48,000 patients by 2035.

Every assumption can be adjusted live in the app: treated fraction, line attrition, real-world PFS, mortality and launch uptake. The source code, evidence register and validation tests are on GitHub.

For current treatment algorithms, see the NCCN guidelines for multiple myeloma.
